# 0006. Identity from the former Supreme Court's facade

Status: accepted, 7 November 2026. Royce picked the facade from five banner drafts, over a recommended block of Acts by decade.

Context. Open Hansard takes its palette from the debating chamber, because people know the chamber from the news and the sitting broadcasts (ADR 0005 in roycehoe/hansard). This project needed the same kind of source for law, and the former Supreme Court was the natural one. Its surviving objects include the copper dome, the Allegory of Justice relief, Court No 1, the Court of Appeal and the strongroom, and openly licensed photos exist for most of them.

Decision. The project's mark draws on the building's facade, and the palette is sampled from photos of the building. The photos are kept in `docs/palette/sources/` for the frontend to use, each under its own licence. The mark may show the building's outline. It shows no State arms, court seal, scales, gavel or figures from the Allegory of Justice relief.

Why. Most Singaporeans, Royce included, have never stepped into the old Supreme Court building. Its interiors are not something the public knows, so few people would recognise a motif taken from a courtroom or a door. The facade is different. It has become an icon in people's minds, and that is what the mark draws on. The test is the one ADR 0005 applied to the chamber, which is what people already recognise, and here it gives the outside of the building rather than the inside.

Consequences.

- A facade alone can be mistaken for any colonial building, so the dome has to read.
- Columns and pediments are common in legal branding. The mark depends on the dome to look like this building.
- The mark encodes no data about the law, unlike openhansard's seat plan, which draws the real seats.
- `docs/palette/research.md` holds interior samples the banner does not use. They stay available to the frontend as supporting colours.
- The README says the photos keep their own licences, because the MIT license covers the code only.

Alternatives.

- A mark built from the law's own data, such as Acts by decade or the sections of one Act. Rejected, because a reader would need the idea explained before it meant anything.
- Colours taken from interior objects, with no building in the mark. Rejected, because few readers would recognise the objects.
- Colours only, to avoid any suggestion of endorsement while the letter to the Supreme Court is open. Not chosen, because the building is now a national monument and part of National Gallery Singapore, and the mark uses no mark of the Court.
